Preface

Preface

Guided by the Party's policy of "letting a hundred flowers bloom, a hundred schools of thought contend," the editor drew on the posthumous work of her late father, Mr. Sun Lutang — The Study of Taijiquan (《太极拳学》) — and on the way he practiced in his later years, and compiled Sun-Style Taijiquan (《孙式太极拳》), published in 1957. To meet the needs of the broad masses in their physical training, the editor has now revised the 1957 edition. The movement descriptions have been amended and expanded, the movement photographs have been retaken, and the applications of certain principal movements have been added, while the original structure has been retained in the main. Although the editor received instruction from her late father in person from early childhood, she is conscious that her own skill falls short of depth and purity; still, for the convenience of those who love Sun-style Taijiquan and wish to practise it, she has done her best.

Confined as it is by the editor's level, the book will inevitably contain errors; readers are earnestly asked to point them out.

— The Editor

Brief Introduction to Sun-Style Taijiquan

Brief Introduction to Sun-Style Taijiquan

The founder of Sun-style taiji (太极, tàijí) quan was Sun Fuquan (孙福全, Sūn Fúquán), courtesy name Lutang (禄堂, Lùtáng), late sobriquet Hanzhai (涵斋, Hánzhāi), a native of Wanxian in Hebei. From childhood he was passionately fond of martial arts. He studied Xingyi Quan (形意拳, Xíngyì Quán) under Li Kuiyuan (李魁垣, Lǐ Kuíyuán), and then studied under Li's own teacher Guo Yunshen (郭云深, Guō Yúnshēn), from whom he received the true transmission. He also studied Baguazhang (八卦拳, Bāguàzhāng) under Cheng Tinghua (程廷华, Chéng Tínghuá). After several years of study and polishing his skill ran deep. He later studied taijiquan under Hao Weizhen (郝为真, Hǎo Wéizhēn). Sun Lutang (孙禄堂, Sūn Lùtáng) devoted his whole life to the study of martial arts; he was proficient in Xingyi, Baguazhang and taijiquan, and gathering the essential meaning of the three schools into one, he created Sun-style taijiquan.

The characteristics of Sun-style taijiquan are: advance and retreat follow one another; when stepping forward one must follow, when stepping back one must withdraw. The movements are extended, rounded and lively, nimble and natural. In practice the two feet are clearly distinguished as empty and full (虚实, xūshí). The whole set, once begun, is like drifting clouds and flowing water, continuous and unbroken. At every turn of the body "opening" and "closing" (开合, kāihé) join, and so it is also called kaihe huobu taijiquan (开合活步太极拳, kāihé huóbù tàijíquán). It is suitable for people of any age and any constitution to practise.

General Explanation of Taijiquan

General Explanation of Taijiquan

Taijiquan is one of the martial arts peculiar to our country. It is an exercise of "cultivating both within and without" (inwardly it governs calming the mind and nurturing the nature, outwardly it governs training the body); it takes suppleness and curvature as its substance and firmness and straightness as its function. Without suppleness and curvature one cannot neutralize; without firmness and straightness one cannot apply. In substance and function it is the fighting method of "using softness to overcome hardness, drawing aside four ounces to deflect a thousand catties." In practising this quan one should sink the qi to the dantian (丹田, dāntián), neither lean nor incline, so that inner and outer join; one must on no account use clumsy strength. One should move the strength by the intent (意, yì) — where the intent arrives, the strength arrives.

As to the method of practising taijiquan, my late father often said: Mr. Hao Weizhen spoke of three levels of mental state in practising taijiquan. At the first stage of practice it is as though the body were in water, both feet treading the ground, and the movements meet the resistance of the water. At the second level it is as though the body were in water with both feet floating up, like a swimmer drifting in the water, able to move freely. At the third level the body is light and nimble, the two feet as if walking on the surface of the water — treading on the brink of the abyss, on thin ice — with the spirit and qi gathered inward, not daring to let there be the slightest scattering or disorder. Then the quan is accomplished.

The postures and movements of taijiquan all have definite essential points, and each has its own meaning. The essentials are introduced in summary below:

Head: The head should push up, but one must not use strength. The chin is naturally drawn in, the top of the head upright, the spirit concentrated. The whole body is relaxed and open; in lifting, pressing, extending and contracting one always uses intent and does not use clumsy strength, and the mind is naturally empty and numinous. This is what is called "empty and numinous, suspending the head-top" (虚灵顶劲, xūlíng dǐngjìn).

Mouth: The mouth should be loosely closed, the tongue touching the upper palate, breathing through the nose.

Chest: The chest should be contained and must not be thrust out. When the chest is contained the qi sinks to the dantian. When the chest is thrust out the qi surges into the chest region, the upper is heavy and the lower light, and the heels float — a thing taboo to the martial practitioner. When the chest is contained the qi clings to the back, and strength issues from the spine — this is true strength. The above is what is called "containing the chest and raising the back" (含胸拔背, hánxiōng bábèi).

Shoulders: Both shoulders must be relaxed and hanging down. One must especially avoid hunching the shoulders, otherwise the qi surges upward.

Elbows: The two elbows should hang loosely downward, the two arms naturally bent. This is the meaning of "seeking the straight within the curved, storing and then issuing."

Hands: The five fingers are spread, the wrist sunk (塌腕, tāwàn), the tiger's mouth slightly rounded, the palm slightly drawn in, as though grasping and holding a ball.

Waist: The waist must be sunk (塌, tā). For the waist is the pivot of the whole body's movements and the source of strength. A person's turning, advancing and retreating, and the changes of empty and full all depend on the waist strength running through them.

Legs: The two legs are bent, and one must clearly distinguish empty and full — that is, the body's centre of gravity is placed on one leg. If the centre of gravity moves onto the right leg, then the right leg is full and the left leg is empty. Conversely, the left leg is full and the right leg is empty. Distinguishing empty and full is the essential meaning of taijiquan; moving thus, the turning is light and nimble. Otherwise the stepping is heavy and sluggish, and one is easily led about by others.

Breathing: What is called "sinking the qi to the dantian" (three inches below the navel) means deep and long abdominal breathing. But one must on no account use strength to press the qi downward; one must simply let the breathing be entirely natural (呼吸, hūxī).

Intent and strength: One of the characteristics of taijiquan is that it uses intent and does not use strength. For taijiquan requires the use of living strength; the whole body must be relaxed and open, not allowing the least bit of clumsy strength to linger between sinews, bones, blood and vessels. It requires being extremely soft and yet extremely firm and hard, extremely heavy and yet extremely nimble. Where the intent arrives the strength arrives, applied with ease. If clumsy strength is used, then it is sluggish and not nimble. If the strength floats on the outside, it does not accord with the requirements of taijiquan. What is meant by using intent and not using strength, and how does living strength arise of itself? It is because where the intent goes the qi arrives; thus the qi and blood flow and pour, day by day infusing and circulating through the channels and network vessels of the whole body, never for a moment stagnating. With long practice true inner strength (内劲, nèijìn) is then produced.

Movement and stillness: The quiet sitting of qigong is seeking movement within stillness; boxing art is seeking stillness within movement. In practising the quan the mind must be still and the spirit concentrated; only then can the movements be rounded and lively.

The above has discussed the essentials of movement separately. But the student must certainly note that taijiquan is an exercise of the whole body, and what is trained lies in the spirit. The spirit is the commander, the body is the servant; if the spirit can be raised, then the movements are naturally light and nimble. Only when mind and intent are in harmony with the movements of the form can inner and outer join as one. In practice one must take care that above and below follow one another, and that all parts of the body are complete and unified. If there is one place where the movement is not whole, the spirit and qi will be scattered and disordered. Furthermore, in practising the quan one should move the strength by the intent, linked without interruption, "like the long Yangtze River, rolling on without cease." "In movement, like drawing silk" —

Containing the chest, one can naturally raise the back;
on no account form "hunchback shoulders."

1. The Push-Hands Method of Taijiquan (太极拳推手法, Tàijíquán Tuīshǒu Fǎ)

Part Two (下编, Xià Biān)

1. The Push-Hands Method of Taijiquan (太极拳推手法, Tàijíquán Tuīshǒu Fǎ)

Every martial art contains two parts, "substance" (体, tǐ) and "application" (用, yòng), and substance and application are mutually connected; Taijiquan is no exception. The various postures of the Taijiquan form are the substance, the art of knowing oneself (知己功, zhī jǐ gōng); although application is present within them, in solo practice it is not easy to experience the methods and the subtle excellences of application, and hence there is Taijiquan push-hands (推手, tuī shǒu) in order to put the art to use.

Taijiquan push-hands takes the practice of application as its main concern and seeks the gateway to effective use. Its methods of use comprise the eight methods (八法, bā fǎ): warding-off (掤, bīng), rolling-back (捋, lǚ), pressing (挤, jǐ), pushing (按, àn), plucking (采, cǎi), rending (挒, liè), elbowing (肘, zhǒu), and leaning (靠, kào); among the eight methods, the four hands (四手, sì shǒu) of warding-off, rolling-back, pressing and pushing are the foundation. In beginning push-hands one must first take the "four hands" as the basic method of practice, drilling them repeatedly and experiencing them with care, until one has trained to the point of adhering, joining, sticking and following (粘连黏随, zhān nián nián suí), neither losing contact nor resisting (不丢不顶, bù diū bù dǐng), with hands and feet nimble and measured, able to judge accurately the magnitude of the opponent's force (劲, jìn); only then can one attain the effects of "leading the opponent in to fall into emptiness" (引进落空, yǐn jìn luò kōng) and "moving a thousand catties with a pull of four ounces" (牵动四两拨千斤, qiān dòng sì liǎng bō qiān jīn). Plucking, rending, elbowing and leaning, and all the various hand techniques, emerge as transformations from within these. At this point one is able to make the whole body insubstantial and numinous, round and lively without obstruction, and infinitely variable — something that brush and ink cannot fully set forth.

The footwork of push-hands comprises static (fixed) step, moving (live) step, joined step, and following step, and so on. A beginner at push-hands should take the static step as the root; only after the static step is proficient does one practise the moving step. In the course of this, joined step and following step may both be used, giving four kinds: static step (joined, following) and moving step (joined, following).

4. The Use of Force and the Listening to Force in Push-Hands (推手的用劲和听劲, Tuī Shǒu de Yòng Jìn hé Tīng Jìn)

In beginning push-hands one must not use clumsy strength, but must seek out the secret within the four words warding-off, rolling-back, pressing and pushing; these four words contain infinite transformations, and the student must experience them deeply.

For example: warding-off has the distinctions of straight warding-off and lateral warding-off, above and below. In rolling back one must stick to the opponent's hand, then immediately change direction and trace a circle, so that the opponent's force cannot issue forth, or if it issues forth is at once dissolved.

Warding-off is divided into upward, downward and level warding-off. In thrusting, one hand must press and hold the opponent's wrist and the other hand press the opponent's elbow, so that the opponent has no room to move and is thrust out along the line of his own force. Again, within the thrust there is also a warding-off force, which should not be used lightly.

Pressing has various methods: straight pressing, slanting pressing, and pressing with the addition of the elbow. If one presses with the arm along a curve, one can change the point of force at any moment and with any configuration, and force may be issued from any place.

Pushing includes lightness and nimbleness or heaviness and solidity, left solid and right empty or right solid and left empty, the two hands opening or closing; in sum, one must change according to the configuration.

When two people drill push-hands together, besides attending to experiencing the methods of using force, one must accomplish bending and extending in accord with the other, neither losing contact nor resisting, not using clumsy strength, calming the mind and listening to force. In time, one can lead the opponent in to fall into emptiness and move a thousand catties with a pull of four ounces.

As for "listening to force" (听劲, tīng jìn), one must on no account mistake it for two parties opposing each other with strength. One commonly sees, in push-hands, two people locking strength against each other, one asking whether the other has force yet, the other answering that he has or has not — this is a great error. "Listening to force" is to sense the magnitude and direction of the opponent's applied force and so change at once; one must on no account lock against it. In beginning practice, train the two arms first; with long practice the whole body can sense it. If one can listen to force, then when the opponent issues force one can dissolve it and make him fall into emptiness. If one cannot listen to force, one need not measure oneself against others; the student must certainly attend to this.

2. The Martial Application of Taijiquan (太极拳技击, Tàijíquán Jì Jī)

The martial application of Taijiquan is nothing other than the eight methods: warding-off, rolling-back, pressing, pushing, plucking, rending, elbowing and leaning. These eight methods are all contained within the Taijiquan form; push-hands is the practising of these eight methods for use. At present, whether one practises the form or practises push-hands, both are the familiarising of these eight methods, and so it is said that the eight methods are the application of Taijiquan. But to practise the form alone is to have substance without application, and its defect lies in having no change. To practise push-hands alone is to have application without substance, and its defect lies in having no root. If one practises only for the cultivation of body and mind, then practising the form is already sufficient; but if one wishes to obtain its application, one must practise both.

In terms of practising substance, Taijiquan is the art of knowing oneself (知己功夫, zhī jǐ gōng fu); only after long and thoroughly proficient practice can one make the whole body insubstantial and numinous, round and lively without obstruction, heavy and still as Mount Tai, light and nimble as a feather, with spirit (神, shén) and vital energy (气, qì) blended and fused, whereby one may of oneself dispel illness and prolong life. In terms of practising application, it is the art of knowing others (知人功夫, zhī rén gōng fu); only after long practice with pure skill can the hands and feet be nimble and alive, leading the opponent in to fall into emptiness and moving a thousand catties with a pull of four ounces. Hence only by practising both substance and application does one come to know oneself and know others; if one cannot know oneself and know others, one cannot measure oneself against others. One commonly sees those who claim to study only "martial application": using force for the sake of using force, they must apply clumsy strength, and so enter a wrong road. The student is asked to guard against this.

To become proficient in the martial application of Taijiquan, one should take the practice of the form as the main thing and seek skill from the waist and legs. For martial application first demands quick reaction; to react quickly one must have each single hand and each single posture thoroughly proficient, with waist and legs nimble and strong, before one can respond to the opponent with lightness, nimbleness, roundness and liveliness. Further, one must be able to "listen to force", for only thus can one stick to the opponent, respond to the moment and adapt, hear one and know a hundred, and change without limit.

In Part One the methods of use of some of the postures have been outlined, but the student must not memorise them by rote and rigidly. One should make the form proficient and practise push-hands diligently. In beginning the static-step push-hands, the work lies mainly in the waist; when the waist is extremely nimble, one has more than enough for dissolving the opponent's force. Once proficient, one practises the moving-step push-hands, where the work lies mainly in the legs; when the skill is pure, one advances, retreats and changes direction swiftly. Thereafter one goes through the four corners, and after that the free hand (散手, sàn shǒu). The two parties may then disregard the rules and attack and dissolve at will, each against the other.

In sum, the martial application of Taijiquan — that is, free hand — has no fixed rule or method, but changes at any time and in any configuration as the opportunity arises; this is the meaning of the saying that free hand has no fixed forms. A single posture in the art may be used in several ways, and several postures may be used as one, but one must always weigh the time and gauge the configuration before acting. VI. Li Yiyu: The Five-Word Formula

Stillness of Heart

If the heart is not still, it is not single-minded: as soon as one raises a hand, front and back, left and right, there is no fixed direction at all — hence the need for stillness of heart. At first one's movements cannot be governed by oneself; one must calm the heart and experience it in the body, moving as the other moves, following the bend and yielding the extension, neither losing contact nor butting, and not shortening or lengthening of oneself. If he has force, I also have force, and my force is first; if he has no force, I also have force, and my intent is still first. One must keep watch at every moment: wherever contact is made, the heart must be applied there, and one must seek the news within "neither losing contact nor butting." Working on from this, with days and months accumulated, one can then apply it in the body; this is entirely the use of intent, not the use of strength, and in time others are controlled by me, and I am not controlled by others.

Nimbleness of Body

If the body is sluggish, one cannot advance and retreat freely — hence the need for nimbleness of body. In raising a hand there must be no appearance of dullness; no sooner does his force begin to intrude upon my skin than my intent has already entered into his bones. VII. Secret Formula of Release and Discharge

The Four Words: Lifting, Drawing, Loosening, Releasing

Lifting opens his energy and borrows his force — within it is the word ling (灵, numinous).

Drawing him to the front of the body, his energy then begins to store — within it is the word lian (敛, gathering).

Loosening my own energy, do not let it bend — within it is the word jing (静, stillness).

At the release, the waist and feet must recognize the target — within it is the word zheng (整, wholeness).

VIII. Essential Words on Practising the Form and Practising the Hands

The men of old said: if one can draw the other in to fall into emptiness, one can then four ounces deflect a thousand catties; if one cannot draw the other in to fall into emptiness, one cannot four ounces deflect a thousand catties. The saying is very summary; the beginner has no way to grasp it. I add several phrases to explain it, so that those who have the will for this art may have a point of entry, and so advance a little each day in achievement. If one wishes to draw [the other] in

If one wishes to know oneself and know the other, one must first give up oneself and follow the other. If one wishes to have no flaw anywhere in the whole body, one must first have spirit and qi drumming and surging. If one wishes to have spirit and qi drumming and surging, one must first raise up the spirit. If one wishes to raise up the spirit, one must first keep the spirit from scattering outward. If one wishes to keep the spirit from scattering outward, one must first gather spirit and qi into the bones. If one wishes to gather spirit and qi into the bones, one must first have strength in the front section of the two legs. The two shoulders loosen and open, the qi sinks downward, energy rises from the heels, exchanges in the legs, is contained in the chest, moves in the two shoulders, and is governed in the waist; above it connects with the two arms, below it follows the two legs, and energy changes from within. Gathering is closing; issuing is opening. When still, all is still; stillness is closing, and within closing is lodged opening. When moving, all moves; movement is opening, and within opening is lodged closing. Touch it, and it revolves freely, with nothing that does not gain force — only then can one draw the other in to fall into emptiness and four ounces deflect a thousand catties. Practice of the form on ordinary days is the work of knowing oneself. At each movement one must first ask oneself whether the whole body accords with these several items. If it accords only slightly not, change it at once. This is why in practising the form one wants slowness, not speed. Push-hands is the work of knowing others; stillness and movement are indeed knowing others, yet it is still a question of asking oneself. If one has arranged oneself well, then when the other touches me, I do not move him the least bit, but enter along his tendency, join and fix his energy, and he falls out of himself. If one has a place where one does not gain force, that is double-weighting not yet transformed, and it must be sought in the opening and closing of yin and yang. This is what is called knowing oneself and knowing the other, and in a hundred battles a hundred victories.
